      A comparative history of infrastructure booms, 1761–2026

      Canals to Compute

      In 2026, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta guided to roughly $720–745 billion of capital spending, most of it for AI data centers, up from about $410 billion in 2025. Measured against the economy, that is already larger than the telecom and fiber build-out at its 2000 peak. It is still well short of the British Railway Mania, when railway construction absorbed 7.3% of GDP in 1847. This site lines the booms up on one scale, explains how every number was built, and lets you push on the assumptions.

      Three things stand out once the booms share an axis. First, the AI build-out arrived faster than any of its predecessors: spending by the four largest buyers roughly quintupled between 2023 and 2026, a pace matched only by the British Railway Mania, where railway investment grew almost ninefold between 1844 and 1847. Second, in level it sits in the middle of the historical range, above canals and fiber, below the railways. Third, its shape is still unresolved. Every earlier boom on this chart turned down within a few years of its steepest acceleration, and the 2027 guidance from Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft points up again.

      Phase clock

      Where each boom stood, year by year

      Carlota Perez divides every technological revolution into an installation period, financed by speculative capital and ending in a crash, and a deployment period financed by the profits of the new industry. Laying the booms on a common clock shows how long each took to go from working prototype to frenzy to bust.

      The gap between demonstration and peak varies a lot, and it has shortened. Canals and the early railways needed fifteen to twenty-five years to reach their manias, because each mile had to be dug or graded by hand and each line needed its own act of a legislature. The telecom boom peaked five years after the commercial internet. The AI build-out is four years past ChatGPT and still accelerating. Speed matters because the gap between building capacity and filling it is where every earlier boom lost money: the British lines authorized in 1845–46 opened in 1847–50, just as traffic per mile fell by a quarter.

      1761 – 1860

      Canals: the first network, and the first public default

      Canals proved that a capital-heavy network could pay for itself, then showed what happens when the imitators build where the traffic is thin.

      The proof of concept

      The Duke of Bridgewater's canal from his Worsley mines toward Manchester opened in 1761 at a cost of about £168,000. Once it reached the town, the price of coal in Manchester roughly halved. The lesson investors took was simple: a waterway that cut haulage costs by an order of magnitude could earn monopoly-like tolls. Over the next seventy years Britain built a network of nearly 4,000 miles.

      The British Canal Mania of the 1790s

      Promotion went from one canal act in 1790 to twenty in 1793, and the capital authorized in a single session rose from £90,000 to about £2.8 million. Some of the lines authorized in those years paid well for decades. Others, such as the Herefordshire and Gloucestershire, never paid a dividend, and the Grand Western was never finished. Estimates rarely survived construction. Opponents of a later Edinburgh–Glasgow railway pointed out that the Union Canal had been projected to cost £246,322 and earn £52,728 a year; it actually cost £448,956 and earned £15,538, a 2.7% return against a promised 20%. By the early 1840s the entire British canal network was capitalized at about £18 million, against £57 million already sunk into railways.

      The Erie Canal and its imitators

      New York built the 363-mile Erie Canal between 1817 and 1825 for about $7.1 million, funded by state taxes and borrowing after Congress declined to pay. It cut the cost of moving a ton of freight between Buffalo and New York City from roughly $100 to about $10, and its tolls repaid the state's construction loans within about nine years. Tolls collected until they were abandoned in 1883 came to about $121 million.

      Success on that scale invited copies. H. Jerome Cranmer's reconstruction for the NBER counts more than 4,000 miles of American canals built between 1815 and 1860 at a cost of about $195 million, 60% of it spent by state governments. He finds three waves. The first, peaking around 1829, produced the profitable lines: the Delaware and Raritan, the Delaware and Hudson, the Lehigh and Schuylkill navigations. The second wave, 1836 to 1844, was larger, more public (almost 70% state-financed) and more western. Canal spending hit $14.3 million in 1840, the high point of the entire era, and fell below $1 million by 1843.

      When the states could not pay

      Indiana's "Mammoth System of Internal Improvements" of 1836 authorized $10 million of 5% bonds at a time when the state's annual revenue was roughly $60,000. The plan only worked if canals were finished and land values kept rising. When the banking house financing Indiana's bonds failed to deliver a payment in 1839, work stopped, and land values that had risen from $5.41 an acre in 1835 to $9.87 in 1837 fell to $3.73 by 1842. In 1841 and 1842 eight states and the Territory of Florida defaulted on their debts; several eventually repudiated part or all of them. Eleven states rewrote their constitutions between 1842 and 1852 to restrict borrowing for internal improvements.

      What the canal era established

      Cranmer draws a distinction that carries through every later boom. An exploitative project served traffic that already existed between two busy places and could pay quickly; private capital would build it. A developmental project was meant to create the traffic that would later pay for it, so profits waited on settlement and growth, and only government would carry it. The Erie Canal was developmental and turned out spectacularly profitable, which persuaded a generation of state legislatures that every developmental canal would. Most were not. Railways then took much of the canals' traffic; British canals began their long decline around 1840.

      1825 – 1850

      The British Railway Mania

      By most measures the largest technology investment boom relative to an economy on record, and one of the best documented.

      A successful mania first

      The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in September 1830. Its promoters had expected to haul cotton and coal; passengers turned out to be the money. The line cost about two and a half times its first estimate and still paid. A smaller mania followed in 1835–37, with Parliament sanctioning about 1,700 miles in three sessions. Those lines also overran their budgets, roughly doubling, yet by the mid-1840s they were judged a success because the professional traffic forecasters of the 1830s had been remarkably accurate. Andrew Odlyzko calls it the rare large mania that looked, in hindsight, as if it had collapsed too early. That success is the key to what followed.

      The Mania

      Railway share prices roughly doubled between 1843 and August 1845. Parliament sanctioned 805 miles of new railway in 1844, 2,700 in 1845 and a record 4,538 in 1846. Railway investment rose from £5 million in 1844 to £43.9 million in 1847, 7.3% of a GDP of about £600 million and close to the entire national government budget of about £50 million. About 250,000 men were building railways in 1847, roughly the size of the British army at the height of the Crimean War.

      The financing was leveraged in a way that is easy to miss. New railway shares were sold for a small deposit, with the rest called in installments as construction proceeded. During the boom, market prices of new railway shares averaged more than twice the capital investors had actually paid in, which amplified returns on the way up. When the calls came, holders had to find cash for lines that were not yet earning, and the calls pushed prices down.

      The forecasting error

      Odlyzko's central argument is that the outcome could have been computed. Total British railway revenue in 1845 was about £6 million, already over 1% of GDP. Share prices implied revenue of around £60 million by 1850–52, which would have made railway revenue about as large as all national taxation. Actual revenue in 1852 was £15 million. The traffic takers who had forecast the 1830s lines so well estimated each proposed line from the road and canal traffic it would capture, without allowing for the dozens of other new lines competing for the same traffic, and revenue per mile of line fell from £2,499 in 1845 to £1,833 in 1848.

      The bust and what it left

      Campbell and Turner's index of railway shares peaked at 1,984 on 8 August 1845 and fell to 673 by 19 April 1850, a decline of about two-thirds. By the end of 1850 investors had put about £250 million into British railways, close to half a year's GDP; Odlyzko estimates their losses at about a third of that. Yet hardly any lines went bankrupt, almost all stayed in service, and Britain came out of the decade with the densest railway network in the world. In 1855 The Economist judged the railways a mechanical triumph and a commercial failure. Fifty years later railway revenue was £109 million a year, about 6% of GDP.

      The scholarly argument

      Historians disagree about whether the Mania was irrational, and the disagreement matters for reading AI.

      Odlyzko: a collective hallucination
      Investors had the data to see that the network could not earn the revenue their prices implied, and the most prominent critics (The Times, The Economist, the MP James Morrison) worried about railways draining capital rather than about railways earning too little, so their warnings may even have encouraged buying.
      Campbell and Turner: informed investors, leveraged instruments
      Shareholder records show few naive investors; insiders put in substantial capital and did no better than other experienced investors. Prices of new lines implied only modest returns on the full investment once the installment structure is accounted for; leverage, more than delusion, drove the swings.
      1830 – 1916

      American railroads: three booms, three busts

      The longest and largest build-out in American history before AI, financed mostly with bonds, much of it with government land and credit.

      Building behind demand, 1830–1857

      Net railroad investment averaged 0.6% of GDP in the 1830s and 0.7% in the 1840s, then 1.7% in the 1850s, reaching about 2.6% in 1854. Albert Fishlow tested Schumpeter's claim that railroads were built "ahead of demand" and found that antebellum lines mostly went into areas that were already settled. His estimate of their social return is high, around 15–20% a year. Speculation in western land tied to new lines and land grants still contributed to the Panic of 1857.

      Building ahead of demand, 1862–1873

      The Pacific Railway Acts of 1862 and 1864 gave the transcontinentals land and federal bonds; land-grant dispositions eventually totaled nearly 160 million acres. About 35,000 miles of track were laid between 1866 and 1873. Richard White's Railroaded argues that these lines were built well before the traffic existed, by promoters who profited through construction companies such as Crédit Mobilier, and that they flooded markets with wheat, cattle and silver nobody needed. When Jay Cooke & Company could not sell Northern Pacific bonds in September 1873, it failed, the New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days, and 89 of the country's 364 railroads went bankrupt. Defaults in 1873–75 reached 36% of the par value of the entire US corporate bond market, a cluster worse than anything in the Great Depression.

      The 1880s and the Panic of 1893

      Building resumed. Railroads absorbed 15–20% of all national investment in the 1870s and 1880s; at times steel rails took 90% of the country's rolled steel. Railroad stock and bond issues in 1880–84 came to about $2.7 billion, around 4.8% of GNP and a quarter of fixed investment. A record 12,876 miles were built in 1887. By 1892 only 44% of railroad shares paid dividends. The Philadelphia and Reading went into receivership in February 1893; the Northern Pacific, Union Pacific and Santa Fe followed, and about a quarter of all railroad mileage ended up in receivership. James J. Hill's Great Northern, finished weeks before the panic and built to carry freight rather than to collect subsidies, stayed solvent.

      Who got the returns

      The network peaked at 254,037 route miles in 1916. In 1900 railroads were 63% of the value of the US stock market and half of Britain's. Alasdair Nairn concludes that, for all their economic impact, railways gave investors negative aggregate returns over the long run. The economic return was large and went mostly to users: Robert Fogel put the 1890 "social saving" at about 2.7% of GNP; Dave Donaldson and Richard Hornbeck estimate that removing the railroads in 1890 would have cut agricultural land values by 60%, a loss of about 3.2% of GNP a year; Hornbeck and Rotemberg estimate an aggregate productivity loss of about 25% and a social return near 45%, roughly ten times the private return.

      1995 – 2003

      Fiber: the glut that built the internet

      The most recent infrastructure bust, the closest in technology to AI, and the one with the clearest record of a demand forecast gone wrong.

      A forecast that compounded

      From 1998, executives at WorldCom and its UUNET unit said internet traffic was doubling every three to four months, growth of about 1,000% a year. Andrew Odlyzko and Kerry Coffman, measuring actual traffic, found it was doubling about once a year. Fast, but nowhere near the claim. The faster number circulated among executives at AT&T, Level 3 and elsewhere and became the planning assumption for a national fiber build.

      The build

      The Telecommunications Act of 1996 opened local markets to new carriers. Capital spending by publicly traded telecom service companies rose from $47 billion in 1995 to about $121 billion in 2000, roughly 1.2% of GDP. Investment in communications equipment more than doubled in real terms between 1996 and 2000. New long-haul networks were financed with high-yield bonds and with loans from the equipment makers themselves: Lucent committed $5.7 billion of credit to customers in 2000, and Nortel's credit commitments to customers stood at $4.1 billion at the end of 2000.

      The glut

      At the same time, wavelength-division multiplexing multiplied the capacity of each strand of glass, so capacity grew even faster than fiber miles. In 2002 TeleGeography estimated that only 2.7% of installed fiber was in use, and bandwidth prices had fallen an average of 65% a year for two years. Equipment makers disputed the figure, arguing that lit circuits were far busier than that. Either way, revenue could not service the debt.

      The bust

      US telecom shares fell about 50% a year for three years after March 2000; an industry that earned more than $20 billion a year in 1995–96 lost a similar amount in 2000–01. More than sixty telecom companies failed. Global Crossing filed in January 2002. WorldCom filed in July 2002 with $107 billion of assets and $41 billion of debt, a month after admitting it had booked $3.8 billion of ordinary line costs as capital spending. About $2 trillion of telecom market value disappeared.

      What it left

      The fiber outlived the companies that laid it. Bought cheaply out of bankruptcy, it became the backbone for search, video streaming and cloud computing over the following decade. Couper, Hejkal and Wolman at the Richmond Fed argue the episode was not a bubble in the strict sense: technology and regulation were changing so fast that forecasts were honestly, hugely uncertain.

      2022 – now

      The AI build-out

      Financed so far by the most profitable companies in history, now shifting toward debt, and running into the power grid as its physical limit.

      Scale and speed

      Combined capital spending at Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta was about $226 billion in 2024 and about $410 billion in 2025. Guidance for 2026 is about $220 billion at Amazon, $195–205 billion at Alphabet, about $170–175 billion at Microsoft and $135–145 billion at Meta. In the July–September 2026 quarter alone Amazon spent $77.3 billion, Alphabet $51.4 billion, Microsoft $45.2 billion including finance leases, and Meta $34.9 billion. Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft have all said 2027 will be higher still.

      How much of this lands in US GDP depends on definitions. Epoch AI, counting AI-related data center construction, compute hardware and networking, puts it at about 0.8% of GDP in early 2026, with all computing infrastructure at about 1.4%, double its 2015–22 average. TS Lombard estimates close to 2% for 2026. Much of the hardware is imported, which is why ING's preferred measure credits the build-out with about a third of 2026 US growth rather than the half that headline numbers suggest.

      From cash flow to debt

      In the third quarter of 2026, operating cash flow exceeded capital spending only at Microsoft. Meta reported negative free cash flow of $2.1 billion, its first in more than a decade. The five largest builders issued about $121 billion of US bonds in 2025 against a 2020–24 average of $28 billion, and about $220 billion in 2026 through August. FactSet estimates new debt rose from 9% of hyperscaler capex in fiscal 2024 to 32% by mid-2026. More sits off the balance sheet: Meta's $27 billion joint venture with Blue Owl for its Hyperion campus, $279 billion of Meta lease obligations not yet begun, and $329 billion of Microsoft data center leases not yet commenced. Credit quality ranges from Microsoft's AAA to Oracle's BBB with a negative outlook.

      Demand so far

      Unlike the fiber glut, capacity is scarce. AWS reports a $578 billion backlog and Google Cloud $561 billion. One-year rental contracts for Nvidia H100s rose from $1.70 to $2.35 an hour between October 2025 and March 2026, and median on-demand rates were about $3.49 in October 2026. OpenAI's annualized revenue was about $50 billion at the end of September 2026; Anthropic's was $65 billion at the end of July on a basis that counts sales through cloud partners. At a fixed level of capability, the price of language-model output has fallen between 9× and 900× a year depending on the task, with a median near 50×.

      The familiar warning signs

      Suppliers are financing customers again: Nvidia agreed to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI, which buys Nvidia systems, and AMD gave OpenAI warrants on its own shares alongside a chip order. Accounting choices flatter the build phase: the hyperscalers have lengthened server lives from four years to five or six since 2020, and Microsoft's move to a 25-year life for data center buildings shifts more future leases out of reported capex. Michael Burry has argued depreciation will be understated by about $176 billion in 2026–28; the companies dispute it. AI-linked stocks are about 45% of the S&P 500; the technology sector alone reached 39.4% in June 2026, against about 35% at the March 2000 peak.

      Workbench

      Models you can push on

      Each model is small enough to explain in a paragraph. The defaults are sourced; every slider says what it means. The historical presets show how the same arithmetic behaved in earlier booms.

      1 · The payback gap: how much AI revenue the installed base needs
      Each year's capital must earn back its cost plus a return over its useful life. The required revenue is compared with a revenue path you set.
      How this is calculated

      Capex by vintage: big-four totals of about $141B (2023), $228B (2024) and $410B (2025), then your 2026 figure growing at your rate through 2030 and flat after. Only the AI share counts. Each vintage is split into short-lived compute and long-lived buildings and power. Each slice needs a level annual payment that repays it with interest over its life (the capital recovery factor, r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n)), for as long as it is in service. The sum is the annual capital charge. Dividing by (1 − operating cost share) gives the revenue the infrastructure owners need. If you count end-user spending, dividing again by (1 − application gross margin) gives what customers of AI products must pay, the step Sequoia's David Cahn uses in his "$600B question". J.P. Morgan's estimate that a 10% return needs about $650 billion of annual revenue is a useful cross-check.

      The model leaves out returns earned inside existing businesses, such as better ad targeting at Meta and Google. Raise "AI revenue today" to include them.

      2 · The depreciation clock: what if GPUs wear out faster than the books say?
      Reported earnings depend on the useful life assigned to servers. Compare the life on the books with the life you think is economic.
      How this is calculated, and the asset-life record

      Each year's server and GPU spending (big-four capex × compute share) is depreciated straight-line over the booked life and, separately, over the economic life. The gap is the earnings difference. Booked lives today: Alphabet 6 years (since 2023), Microsoft 6 (2022), Oracle 6 (2025), Meta 5.5 (2025), Amazon cut a subset from 6 to 5 years in 2025. Nvidia says A100s shipped six years ago still run at full utilization; one analysis puts physical life at seven to nine years and payback at two to four.

      For comparison: the Erie Canal still carries boats two centuries after it opened; almost all British Mania lines stayed in service; much fiber laid in 1998–2001 is still in use. The AI build-out is the first great infrastructure boom whose main asset will be obsolete within one business cycle.

      3 · The absorption race: how long a glut lasts
      Utilization moves with demand growth, divided by the growth of capacity from new building and from efficiency gains that let the same assets do more.
      How this is calculated, and how well it fits history

      Utilization in year t = starting utilization × ((1 + demand growth) ÷ ((1 + net capacity growth) × (1 + efficiency gain)))t. Above 100% means shortage.

      British railways, 1848. Revenue per mile stood at 73% of its 1845 level. Revenue grew about 10.6% a year to 1856 while mileage grew about 6.3%. The model returns to 100% in about eight years, and the observed series (dots) did so in 1856. Long-haul fiber, 2002. 2.7% of installed fiber in use; traffic doubling yearly; per-strand capacity improving with wavelength multiplexing. The model reaches 50% around 2010, consistent with the common account that the glut took most of a decade to absorb. AI. Starting near full use, the outcome turns on whether effective demand for compute keeps outgrowing new capacity plus algorithmic efficiency. Small changes in either rate swing the answer from shortage to glut within three years.

      4 · Base rate: how often do sharp run-ups crash?
      Greenwood, Shleifer and You studied 40 US industry run-ups of more than 100% in two years, 1926–2014. Set an industry's two-year return net of the market.
      Crash = a drawdown of 40% or more within two years. Unconditional rate about 14%. Run-ups did not predict lower average returns, and in run-ups that did crash, prices peaked on average six months after first qualifying, rising another 30% first. This is a historical frequency, not a forecast.

      Synthesis

      What rhymes and what breaks

      Six patterns recur across all four earlier booms and are visible again today. Six features of the AI build-out have no clean precedent.

      Rhymes

      1. Forecasts extrapolated from the best early case. The traffic takers applied a method calibrated on trunk lines to branch lines; WorldCom's three-month doubling came from one network's early years. Today, revenue run-rates are reported on incompatible bases: OpenAI's widely reported $70 billion run-rate was a grossed-up figure built to match Anthropic's method, and on OpenAI's own basis it was about $50 billion.
      2. Suppliers lending to customers. Lucent and Nortel financed carriers who bought their gear; Nvidia, AMD and the clouds now take stakes in labs that buy their chips and capacity.
      3. Accounting that flatters the building years. George Hudson paid railway dividends out of capital; WorldCom capitalized line costs; today's debate is over server lives and lease classification.
      4. Funding drifts to borrowed money late in the cycle. British share calls in 1846–48, junk bonds in 1999–2000, hyperscaler debt rising from 9% to 32% of capex since 2024.
      5. The build-out props up the wider economy. British railway construction employed as many men as the army; US railroads took 15–20% of national investment; AI investment accounts for roughly a third of 2026 US growth.
      6. Many builders racing for the same traffic. Parallel British lines, duplicate American trunk lines, dozens of competing local carriers; today four hyperscalers, Oracle, several GPU clouds and the labs themselves are all building frontier capacity.

      Breaks

      1. Who pays. Earlier booms were funded by individual shareholders, state treasuries or leveraged start-ups. Most AI capex still comes from companies with hundreds of billions of operating cash flow, so a bust would land first on equity valuations and on the weaker links (neoclouds, SPVs, Oracle) rather than on bank balance sheets.
      2. Capacity is scarce, not idle. Fiber was 2.7% lit; GPU rental prices rose in 2026. The British railway glut appeared only after lines opened, though, so tightness today does not rule out a glut once 2026–27 capacity arrives.
      3. The main asset is short-lived. Canals, railbeds and glass lasted decades. A GPU is written off in five or six years. A bust would leave less durable infrastructure behind, and an overbuild would also clear faster.
      4. The output deflates at unprecedented speed. Per-token prices at fixed capability fall roughly tenfold or more each year. Users capture that surplus, as railway shippers did; the builders need volume to grow faster than price falls.
      5. Power is a hard brake. Data centers' share of US peak summer demand is projected to double from 4.1% to 8.5% between 2025 and 2027, and interconnection takes years. Parliament's committee rooms played a similar rationing role for British railways.
      6. Concentration. Four companies account for most of the spending. Railways had hundreds of companies. Few buyers can coordinate a slowdown quickly, or keep spending for strategic reasons long after the returns turn poor.
      The most consistent finding across 260 years is about who captures the value. In every earlier case the network was built, used and economically transformative, while most of the investors who financed the peak years lost money and the gains went to users and to later owners who bought the assets cheap.

      Method

      How the numbers were built

      Why share of GDP

      Dollar comparisons across two centuries are meaningless; a share of the economy's annual output asks how much of a society's current production was diverted into the new network. It is also the standard used by Odlyzko, Kedrosky and others. It has limits: nineteenth-century GDP is reconstructed, investment was measured differently, and gross spending, net capital formation and securities issued are three different things. Each series below says which it is.

      Series by series

      • UK railways. Railway capital investment ÷ UK GDP, both from Odlyzko's Table 1, which draws on Mitchell and modern reconstructions. GDP covers the United Kingdom including Ireland.
      • US canals. Cranmer's annual canal construction spending ÷ MeasuringWorth nominal GDP. GDP for 1822–26, 1832–36 and 1842–46 is interpolated geometrically; the 1842–46 interpolation probably overstates GDP in a deflationary period, so the true share may be slightly higher.
      • US railroads. No continuous annual series exists. Sourced benchmarks: 0.2% (1830), 0.9% (1839) and 2.6% (1854) net investment from Fishlow via Pereira et al.; construction cost about 4% of GNP in 1880, 3% in 1887 and 2.5% in 1890 (Haines et al. via a Banco de México study); railroads at 20% of gross capital formation in the 1870s and 7.5% in the 1890s (Ulmer); securities issued equal to 4.8% of GNP in 1880–84. Years between benchmarks are the author's interpolation, shaped by miles built (peaks in 1871–72, 1881–82 and 1887). Treat the line as ±1 percentage point.
      • US telecom and fiber. Capex of publicly traded telecom service companies ($47B in 1995, $121B in 2000) ÷ GDP; 1996–99 interpolated; 2001–03 scaled by RHK's estimates of the decline.
      • AI, broad. Big-four capex ÷ US GDP. This overstates the AI share (it includes Amazon logistics and spending abroad) and understates the total (it omits Oracle, GPU clouds, labs and corporate buyers). 2020–23 values are approximate calendar-year totals from company filings; 2024–25 from ValueAdd; 2026 is the guidance midpoint ($732B) over an estimated $31.6 trillion GDP.
      • AI, narrow. Epoch AI's estimate of AI-related data center construction, compute hardware and networking at about 0.8% of GDP in Q1 2026.

      Tracker gauges and warning bands

      Bands mark ranges that earlier booms reached on their way into distress: utilization below about 75% (Britain 1848, fiber 2002), capacity prices falling more than 30% a year (fiber), three-year capex growth above 3× (both manias), external financing above half the build (canals, British railways), and so on. They are descriptive, drawn from four or five cases, and should be read as "this is where trouble happened before," never as thresholds with predictive power.

      What this site does not claim

      It does not forecast a crash. Several episodes look better in hindsight than they did at the time: the British railway mania of the 1830s was judged a success within a decade, and the fiber glut became cheap infrastructure for the next economy. Greenwood, Shleifer and You find that sharp run-ups do not predict lower average returns even though they predict higher crash odds.
